Chapter 02 · Rain

Rain is easy to see. The surge that follows is not.

A storm can soak a basin for an afternoon and leave the river looking unchanged.

What matters happens underground. Dry soil drinks the water. Saturated soil sends it straight into the channel, and the river rises faster than anyone watching the sky expected.

Chapter 03 · The river rises

The river rises where three streams meet.

FLOODCAST reads three public sources and merges them into one daily picture per catchment.

USGS

Observed flow

Discharge from stream gauges, in cubic feet per second (cfs). The quantity the model learns to predict.

Open-Meteo

Weather and ground

Daily rainfall, temperature and soil moisture, the forces that decide how much water runs off.

StreamStats

Catchment shape

Drainage area, slope and land cover, which set how quickly water travels to the channel.

Chapter 05 · The peak

Peaks are scored hardest, because they matter most.

Most days on a river are ordinary, and an average error hides that.

The few days at the crest decide whether a town is warned in time. FLOODCAST reports extreme-event error on its own line, so a model that is good on quiet days cannot quietly fail at the top of the flood.

Chapter 07 · After the storm

After the storm, the work goes on.

Limitations

  • Daily averages can flatten flash peaks that last only hours.
  • Ungauged rivers rely on catchment attributes, so error grows where those are sparse.
  • Input forecasts carry their own uncertainty, and predictions do not yet show a range.
  • Dams, levees and urban drainage are not modeled explicitly.

Future scope

  • Sub-daily timesteps for flash-prone basins.
  • Uncertainty ranges on every estimate.
  • Live ingestion of rainfall forecasts.
  • Wider station coverage for regional calibration.
